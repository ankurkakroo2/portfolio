---
heading: See the Problem as It Is
---
One of the rarest skills I've seen in people is the ability to look at a problem without seeing themselves in it. Not their title, not their skill set, not the thing they're best at. Just the problem.

Most of us don't do this. Give an engineer a problem and you'll get an architecture diagram before the problem is fully understood. Give it to a PM and you'll get a PRD. An analyst will come back with a dashboard. None of these are wrong. They're just answers shaped by the person holding the pen. The problem gets translated into whatever language we're fluent in, and some of it gets lost in translation.

Then comes the expensive part. Somebody has to stitch everybody's output together into something that actually solves the original problem. Anyone who has sat in that room knows how hard this is. Every perspective is partially right, nobody owns the whole thing, and the end result is often a well-engineered answer to a slightly different question.

Specialization made sense when doing the work was the hard part. You needed someone who could write the code, someone who could size the market, someone who could model the numbers. Your lens was the price of being useful.

AI is changing that price. It lifts the level of abstraction so much, and empowers everybody so much, that the doing is no longer the bottleneck. An engineer can reason about the business. A PM can prototype. An analyst can ship. When everybody can do a bit of everything, the habit of solving a problem through the lens of your specialization stops being a strength. It's just a bias with a job title.

What's left is the part that was always the hardest: seeing the problem clearly in the first place. What is actually broken? For whom? What would "solved" look like, independent of how any of us would like to solve it?

I think this becomes the defining skill for builders. Not the best coder, not the best spec writer. The person who can look at a problem as it is, pick whichever tools fit, and not care much whether those tools match their résumé. Engineers and builders who can do this will be more valuable than ever.

It's an uncomfortable shift. A lot of us built our identity around our specialization. But the problem never cared what we were good at. It's high time we stopped caring too.
